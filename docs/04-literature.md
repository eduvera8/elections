# Annotated reading list

Flagged `[UNREAD]` where the full text has not been read. Everything in this file was compiled
without network access to the papers themselves, from abstracts and secondary summaries.
**Verify before citing.**

## Read first

**De la Calle (2024), "When rebels lose: The impact of civil war legacies on contemporary
electoral outcomes in Peru, 2011–2021," *Electoral Studies* 90:102827.** `[UNREAD]`
Open access (CC BY); copies at Warwick WRAP (eprint 190657) and White Rose (eprint 233457).
The direct precedent and the paper to beat. District-level, first rounds of 2011/2016/2021,
Keiko plus three left candidates, key regressor = Sendero deaths relative to security-force
deaths, plus Sendero territorial presence. Finds a Fujimori premium and a left penalty in
conflict-affected districts, robust to crime, poverty, COVID-19 deaths and long-run
marginalisation; Castillo partly offsets it in 2021 in poor and COVID-hit districts.
**Read this before writing anything else, and revise `01-research-design.md` accordingly.**

## Conflict legacies in Peru

- **Soifer & Vergara (eds.), *Politics after Violence: Legacies of the Shining Path Conflict in
  Peru*** (University of Texas Press, 2019). `[UNREAD]` The canonical volume. Sections on
  institutions, parties, and public opinion/civil society. Chapters to prioritise: Muñoz on the
  defeat of the left; Maldonado, Merolla & Zechmeister on public opinion. Reviewers note the
  editors concentrate on 1980–1994.
- **León (2012), "Civil Conflict and Human Capital Accumulation: The Long-Term Effects of
  Political Violence in Perú," *Journal of Human Resources*.** `[UNREAD]` **Methodologically the
  most important item on this list after De la Calle** — the district × birth-cohort exposure
  design this project should borrow for Route B. Verify the exact citation.
- Feinstein, on the conflict and the legal left. `[UNREAD]`

## Fujimorismo and the party system

- **Schady (2000), "The Political Economy of Expenditures by the Peruvian Social Fund
  (FONCODES), 1991–95," *APSR* 94(2):289–304.** `[UNREAD]` The FONCODES confound. Province-level;
  spending rose before elections and was steered where it would have most political effect.
  Check whether conflict intensity enters as a separate variable — the summaries do not say.

## Comparative — violence, disaster and voting

Useful for framing the blame-attribution argument. The nearest parallel debate is the one on
COVID-19 mortality and voting, where Falcão, Rodrigues & Abrucio (2026, *Journal of Politics in
Latin America*) find Brazilian voters punished the president but not governors for pandemic
excess mortality — i.e. blame attribution is actor-specific, which is exactly the claim this
project tests for 1980s violence. Also: the French literature finding incumbents *rewarded* in
hard-hit areas, and the US co-partisan reward / out-partisan punishment results.

## Gaps this project targets

1. No study covers 1995, 2000, 2001 or 2006 at district level with conflict intensity.
2. No study covers 2026.
3. No study separates rondas campesinas/CADs from army violence.
4. No study tests age/cohort heterogeneity or cohort-replacement decay.
5. No study uses blank/null voting as an outcome.
6. No quantitative measurement of terruqueo's electoral effect.
